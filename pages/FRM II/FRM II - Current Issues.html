1.2. Answering the 6 Questions

8 Events Leading up to Failure of SVB
  • unique business model
  • rapid growth
  • low interest rate environment
  • investment in securities (long durations)
  • rising interest rates
  • deposit outflow: tech company faces financial pressure
  • publicity and panic
  • failure and government intervention
10 Shortfalls in Oversight of SVB from RBO to LFBO
  • inadequate risk assessment: about unique business model
  • oversight transition
  • interest rate risk management
  • liquidity risk supervision
  • governance and risk management: wrong incentive compensation
  • supervision committee focus: lack of attention
  • horizontal perspective and broader risk issues
  • effective supervision and examiner training
  • impact of rising rates on AOCI and FHLB borrowing
  • regulatory framework and tailoring
7 Failures of SVB in Governance and Risk Management
  • ineffective risk management framework
  • CRO position
  • internal audit function
  • board of director's oversight
  • incentive compensation policies: encourage excessive risk-taking for short-term target
  • lack of independent challenge
  • risk appetite and tolerance: aggressive
6 Specifc Risk Issues Leading to SVB's Failure
  • deposit concentration: technology sector
  • type of deposits: non-maturity deposit
  • held-to-maturity securities
  • available-for-sale securities: fair value not recorded on the income statement
  • contingent funding plan and capacity
  • capital raising effort
    urgency and scale of capital result in panic
7 Failures of SVB in Liquidity
  • inadequate stress testing
  • 30 day liquidity buffer: insufficient
  • contingent funding plan
  • liquidity risk management governance
  • realized and unrealized losses
    more attention to unrealized loss from AFS securities, lacking attention on liquidity
  • market and funding risk assessment
  • regulatory reporting and monitoring
5 Deficiencies in SVB's Interest Rate Risk Management
  • modeling process: do not reflect sensitivity of asset liability to interest rate
  • duration management
  • hedges and derivatives: fail to hedge
  • interest rate scenario analysis: ignoring extreme scenarios
  • risk appetite and limits

2 metrics could improve interest rate management

  • net interest income at risk
  • economic value of equity

2. The Credit Suisse CoCo Wipeout: Facts, Misperceptions, and Lessons for Financial Regulation

Introduction to Credit Suisse:

  • second large bank in Swiss, first lager is UBS
  • operation deterioration

CoCo bond as additional tier 1 capital

2.1. Features of CoCo Bonds (**)

  • loss absorption mechanism
    • converted to common equity
    • principal write-down
  • trigger
    • mechanical: capital ratio, so called point of non-viability (PONV)
    • discretionary
      Basel III require a discretionary PONV trigger

under Basel III, CoCos must satisfy two requirements to qualify as regulatory capital

  • PONV trigger
  • going-concern rule, minimum trigger level of CET1/RWA to qualify as AT1 is 5.125%

2.2. Rationale for Banks to Issue CoCos

  • raise capital while limiting dilution cost of current shareholders and reduce likelihood of insolvency or requiring government bailout
  • cost of bank failure are borne by investors rather than taxpayers

2.3. Compare Rescue of Credit Suisse to That of Bear Stearns

Credit Suisse

  • merger
  • write down Credit Suisse
Dimension Credit Suisse Bear Stearns
Ways merger (write-down liability) purchase and assumption
Write Down no yes
Tax Payer at Risk or Not investor of CoCos collateralized SPV (Federal Reserve and Treasury department), putting public money at risk

2.4. Lessons

  • difference between going-concern and gone-concern
    transfered to equity or write-down
  • contractual complexity increases risk of misrepresentation
    giving regulators considerable ex ante discretion
  • due diligence before investing in CoCos
  • reverse convertible CoCo design eliminating involvement of regulator in conversion decision
    no PONV

2.5. Conclusions

  • of all too-big-to-fail tools, CoCos play intended role
  • regulator need to review the design

C. Climate Risk

6. Climate-Related Risk Drivers and Their Transmission Channels

paper in 2021

6.1. Climate-Related Risk Drivers

  • physical risk drivers: weather change
    • acute: extreme change
    • chronic: ocean acidification
  • transition risk drivers: transition tolow-carbon economy
    • climate policies
    • technology
    • investor sentiment
    • consumer sentiment

6.2. Transmission Channels

how climate risk drivers affect financial risk

  • microeconomic: specifc counterparty or financial asset
  • macroeconomic: macro factor

microeconomic

  • on credit risk
    • real estate damaged → LGD increase
    • corporate profitablity decrease → PD increase
    • sovereign's tax decrease → PD increase
  • on market risk
    • physical: uncertainty of climate physical risk → asset volatility increase
    • transition: change borrowing cost and an abrupt repricing
  • on liquidity risk
    • liquidity needs increase
  • on operational and reputational risk
    • telecommunications infrastructure disrupt
    • financing carbon related corporates

macroeconomic

  • on credit risk
    • physical: huge disaster recovery cost → higher tax, lower government spending and reduced economic activity → credit risk
    • transition: negative impact for government revenue / higher corporate production cost

6.3. Amplifiers and Mitigants

Amplifier:

  • Interactions and interdependencies between climate risk drivers
    • extreme weather → low carbon policy
  • Financial risk amplifier
    • climate change → higher insurance premium → more risk become uninsurable → amplify climate risk
  • Risk drivers transmitted through multiple channels
    • individual wealth → bank credit risk → macroeconomy deteriorate → individual wealth decline

Mitigants:

  • Proactive actions
    • diversification
  • Reactive actions
    • hedging

Mitigants:

  • bank behavior and business model
    • reduce climate related exposure
    • calibrate credit model to capture climate risk
    • securitization of climate related exposure
    • support disaster affected areas
  • availability and pricing of insurance
    • insurance-linked security (implicit insurance bond)
  • depth and maturity of capital markets and opportunities for securitization
    • sell mortgage
  • hedging opportunities
    • derivatives

7. Climate-Related Financial Risks - Measurement Methodologies

7.1. Conceptual Consideration

  • physical risk measurement:
    • damage function: impact of specific hazard on real asset and activities
  • transition risk measurement: model link transition risk drivers to economic factor
  • considered jointly: increase physical risk tends to put pressure on transition risk

exposure granularity:

  • computational complexity
  • data
  • business requirement

top-down and bottom-up approaches to measure risk
incorporating risk mitigation and risk reduction: net exposure and gross exposure
heterogeneities (of corporate) play a key role in selecting measurement approaches
uncertainty may lead to misestimation of risks

7.2. Data Needs

  • data describing physical and transition risk drivers
    • commercial, academic, governmental
  • data describing the vulnerability of expusures
    linking climate-adjusted economic risk factors to exposures
    • geospatical data for corporates
    • location data for mortgage
    • couterparty's sensitivity on climate
  • financial exposure data
    translate climate-adjusted economic risk factor to financial risk
    • data used to project cash flows or prices

7.3. Measurement Approaches

  • Integrated Assessment Model (IAM)
    combine approaches with economic growth modeling
    lack resiliency to unforseen endogenous events
    IAM does not model money, finance, or banking
  • Input-Output Model
    static linkage, upstream and downstream impact of shocks to a given industry
  • Computable General Equilibrium (CGE)
    structural policy studies
  • Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE)
    computationally intensive
  • Overlapping Generation Model (OLG)
    long-term macroeconomic evolutions
    assumes individuals have perfect foresight
  • Agent-Based Model (ABM)
    micro level, agent interactions

Broad risk measurement approaches:

  • climate risk scores or ratings
  • scenario analysis
  • stress testing
  • sensitivity analysis
  • natural capital analysis: posits nature as a capital stock
  • climate value at risk

7.4. Methods for Exposure Mapping and Measurement

banks:

  • physical risk: measure risk at portfolio level
  • client or project ratings and scores

supervisors:

  • for real estate exposures, transition risk analyses are fewer
  • assess risk in financial system, vulnerability of bank's business model

7.5. Gaps and Challenges in Future Exploration

exposure mapping

  • identification criteria not granular enough
  • comparability reduce risk differentiation

design modeling framework:

  • uncertainty
  • time horizon related challenge: up to 2050

potential policy actions to mitigate crypto risks:

E. Digital Resilience

10. Digital Resilience And Financial Stability. The Quest For Policy Tools In The Financial Sector

10.1. Digital Resilience

  • cyber risk: cyber security, CIA (confidentialilty, integrity, accessability)
  • ICT (Information and Communication Technology) risk: operational disruptions

Evolution Features of Cyber Attacks

  • smart latency: time between system breaches and identification, materialization and neutralization
  • percolation: cyber risk propagation

interplay between cyber risks and financial stability (liquidity, leverage and trust)

10.2. Macroprudential Tools

  • cyber free rider (concentration of cyber service)
  • cyber epidemics and confidence breakdowns
  • short-sighted view of risks to cyber shocks
  • cloud contagion

Singular nature of cyber and ICT risks rules out most ordinary macroprudential tools.

  • ordinary
    • countercyclical capital buffer
    • exposure limit to digital risk factors
  • new
    • circuit breaker: temporary break
    • cooperative arrangement:
      prisoner's dilemma: selfish competition, the balance resorts to public authorities
      collective ICT buffers (public computing resources for emergency)
    • systemic technology provider and perimeter of macroprudential policy
      penalize concentrated use of same software
