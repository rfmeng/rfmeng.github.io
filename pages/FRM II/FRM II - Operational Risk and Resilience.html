FRM II - Operational Risk and Resilience

Modified 2024-10-31 Categorized FRM II

A. Holistic Overview of Operational Risk and Resilience

1. Introduction to Operational Risk and Resilience

1.1. Definition of Operational Risk by BCBS

Basel Committee On Banking Supervision (BCBS)

Risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed processes, people and systems or from external events (PPSE).
The definition includes legal risk, but excludes strategic and reputational risk.
Where approprite, strategic and reputational risk should be considered as operational risk.

Categories of Operational Risk Events under Basel II
  • Internal fraud (IF)
    unauthorized activities, theft and fraud, embezzlement, misappropriation of assets, bribe
  • External fraud (EF)
    theft and fraud (check kitting and forgery), systems security (hacker attack)
  • Employment practices and workplace safety (EPWS)
    more prominent in America, there is more a cultrue of litigation against the employers
    • employee relations
    • safe enviroment
    • diversity and discrimination
  • Clients, products, and business practices (CPBP)
    disputes with clients, regulatory fines from improper business practices
    client misinformation, complaints an discounts due to errors, product misspecification
  • Damage to physical assets (DPA)
    natural disaster and terrorism
    very few firms collect loss on this rick type, as these are usually too small or too large. Senario analysis and insurance
  • Business disruption and system failures (BDSF)
    IT breakdown, outages (electricity)
  • Execution, delivery, and process management (EDPM)
    failed transaction processing: processing errors, missing documentation, vendor disputes
Frequency and Severity of Seven Events

CPBP consititues more than half of loss from less than one quarter of frequency.
EF, EPWS, CPBP, EDPM contributes most of risk events.
CPBP and EDPM are to explain most of the loss.
just direct financial losses, no reputational damage, market capitalization, the latter far exceeds the former.

1.2. Characteristic of Operational Risk ***

  • heterogenous: different
  • idiosyncratic and diffuse: could not be totally hedged
  • heavy tailed: large number of small loss and small number of large loss
  • interconnected: share common internal cause (weak internal control etc.)
  • dynamic: operational risk exposure evolve with business activities

1.3. Operational Risk Management (ORM) Frameworks

  • governance (assignment of rols and responsibilities)
  • risk identification → risk appetite → risk assessment → risk mitigation → risk monitoring
  • conduct and culture
    ORM cycle: risk identification → risk assessment → risk mitigation → risk monitoring
    risk appetite is determined by board of directors

1.4. Operational Resilience

ability to recover from operational disruptions

OR in UK

the ability to prevent, adapt to, respond to, recover from, and learn from operational disruptions
impact tolerance level:

  • amount of disruption that could be tolerated
  • must contain a time-based metric, similar to recovery time
OR in USA

ORM is central component
ORM has 2 specializations: third-party risk management, scenario analysis
Operational resilience has 2 critical supporting pillar: business continuity management, IT systems resilience

2. Risk Governance

2.1. BCBS Principles for the Sound ORM (***)

  • culture led by board of directors and implemented by senior management
  • ORM framewrk
  • senior management responsibility
    • board review and approval of ORMF
    • risk appetite and tolerance statement
    • senior management role in ORM
  • risk mangement process
    • identification and assessment of operational risk
    • management process
    • monitoring
    • control environment
  • robust information and communication technology (ICT) management program
  • business continuity plan
  • public disclosure

2.2. Supervisory Expectation on Governance of ORM

maintain a library of regulatory documents, operational risk staff should be asked to confirm their awareness

2.3. Committee Structure in Risk Governance

  1. business lines operational risk committee: oversee business activity
  2. operational risk committee: oversee, monitor and report to higher level
  3. organization risk committee: oversee, monitor and report to higher level
  4. risk committee of the board: oversee all operational risk

2.4. Role of BOD in Risk Governance

ultimately responsible

2.5. Three Lines of Defense Model (***)

  • Line 1: business unit management
  • Line 2: independent corporate operational risk management (法律合规部)
  • Line 3: internal audit (稽核) report to non-executive directors

The first line owns the final sign-off on risk assessment and controls. The first line owns the risk and the second line owns the methodology.

2.6. Risk Appetite

nature and extent of significant risks it is willing to take
Board of directors delegates the responsibility to own the risk limit to risk committee.

Risk Appetite Governance

Risk owners are the first line of defense.
Risk owners designate control owners and metric owners.

Regulatory Expectations

2.7. Risk Culture

  1. codes of conduct and ethics: no-blame culture, tone from top
  2. compensation structure
    agrresive compensation strucutre (increase proportion of incentive compensation for traders and investment bankers) not recommended
  3. training to raise awareness of operatinal risks
Signs of Good Risk Culture

includes swift escalation of issues

3. Risk Identification

3.1. Scope of Risk Indentification

reconciliation of top-down and bottom-up

Top-down

outcome: risk prioritization and risk ranking

Tools
  • business-specific risk indentification: exposure and vulnerability
  • risk wheel
    domino effect: salary cut down → personal effectivenss → systems disruption
  • horizon scanning: for nascent emerging risk
    political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental (PESTLE)
Bottom-up
Tools
  • Event and loss data analysis
    • internal losses
    • external losses: consortium data
    • near misses: incidents does not result in loss luckily
  • risk and control self-assessment (RCSA)
    facilitated by second line, collect information from first line
    from most experienced employees and recent hires
    typically annually, and after trigger event
    if too granular, the output will be small
  • process mapping: what can go wrong in each step of a process
    too granular vs. too high-level

3.2. Scenario Analysis

brainstorming techniques for extreme events

  • preparation phase: minimize subjective biase
    participants: senior managers from different business functions
    external experts
  • generation phase
  • intermediary phase: consolidate and eliminate
    some scenarios can be eliminated if maximum loss is moderate to be absorbed by operating margin

3.3. Operational Risk Taxonomy

Basel Taxonomy (***)
  • level 1: 7 categories
  • level 2: definition (detailed version of level 1)
  • level 3: examples

leve 1 and level 2 are regulatory categories

Operatioanl Risk Data Exchange (ORX)

revises Basel

Structure of Taxonomies

cause and control are prime objects
Eliminate the downside of a risk while preserving its upside makes sense. (find cause and control)

Cause Taxonomy

PPSE

Impact Taxonomy

direct financial impact, indirect financial impact, non-financial impact

Control Taxonomy

preventative, detective, corrective, directive

4. Risk Measurement and Assessment

ORM framework: OR incident database → RCSA → KRI → Lessons learned

4.1. Incident Database

Comprehensiveness of Data

Basel committee does not difine material exposuresm, but set minimum threshold for loss reporting
"cherry-picking": biased selection

regulatory perspective: report financial loss only
management perspective: also record non-financial loss, which will result in financial loss

grouped loss: OR events connected through a common cause

Dates of Incidents

occurrence, discovery, reporting, accounting (when financial impact enters General Ledger, GL)
risk-based approach: material incidents must be reported sooner, minor incidents can be included in periodic summary reporting

Data Quality Requirement

reconsiling the loss data
GL is no substitue for incident database because it only records direct financial loss

4.2. Qualitative Risk Assessment

Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA)

inherent risks, residual risks (risk remained after control)
workshop-style discussions, questionaire

  • Severity Assessment: Impact Scales
    financial, regulatory, customer, reputation
  • Likelihood Assessment Scales
    likelihood or frequency1
  • heatmap to link likelihood and severity
Outcome of RCSA

results should be documented, submitted for approval and sign-off

Challenge of RCSA

difficult to be comparable

KPIs, KRIs and KCIs (***)
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

error rates on retail transactions

Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)

examples of KRIs measuring likelihood:

  • increase in number of trasactions per staff
  • drop in customer satisfaction scores (client attrition)
  • increase in level of sales required to achieve a performance objective (fraud)

examples of KRIs measuring impact:

  • increase in level of proprietary company knowledge held by a key employee
  • increase in sensitivity of data held on a given server
  • increase in value generated by top-10 clients
Key Control Indicators (KCIs)
  • missed due diligence items
  • lack of segregation of duties
  • overlooked errors in four-eye checks (reviews by two competent individuals)

Three indicators overlap, the failure of a control function is jointly KPI, KRI, KCI.

4.3. Quantitative Risk Assessment (***)

Causal Analysis of Scenarios

forward-looking

Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

decompose failure into a series of external conditions and internal conditions.
calculate likelihood based on logic tree.

FAIR Methodology

factor analysis of information risk

Root-Cause Analysis and Bowtie Tool

5 why analysis, "why did this happen"
cause → event → impact, it looks like a bowtie

Swiss Cheese: Control Layering

cumulative act model, each defensive layer is like a slice of Swiss cheeses.
The presence of one hole causes bad outcomes when holes in many layers momentarily line up.
assessing independence of controls is at least important as assessing reliability of each individual control.

4.4. Operational Risk Modeling and Capital (***)

loss distribution approach (LDA): Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA)

  • frequency: modeled with Poisson and negative binomial (probability of k failures before r sucesses)
  • severity: lognormal, Weibull and Generalized Pareto (GPD, more heavy tailed)

Severity and frequency are assumed to be independent, and convoluted into an aggregated loss distribution by Monte Carlo.
mix internal and external data: scale, cut-off mix (threshold), filter
losses in each risk classes are not iid.

4.5. Quantificaion of Operational Resilience

OR focuses on the continuity and recovery of important business service, while business continuity management focuses on each business.
single points of failure (SPOF): back-up and redundancy for key employees with unique knowledge and skill.

5. Risk Mitigation

External OR cannot be prevented, but they can be reduced.
Internal OR are more preventable through internal control.

5.1. Risk Response

  • tolerate: accpet risk
  • treat: risk migitation
  • transfer: move to another party (insurance)
  • terminate: cease operation

5.2. Internal Controls: Types and Testing

Internal Control Types (***)
  • preventive controls: reduce likelihood of an incident
    • segregation of duties
    • access controls
    • levels of authorization
    • process automation
  • detective controls:
    • exception report
    • file reconciliation
    • smoke detector
    • intrusion detection system
  • corrective controls:
    • data back-ups
    • IT systems patch management
    • continuity planning
    • systems redundancy
  • directive controls: rules and prescriptions
    • procedures and guidance manuals
    • internal training
    • team supervision
    • work handover procedures
Control Design

weakly designed control:

  • optimistic: sign-off for large volume of documents before a deadline
  • collective: "four-eyes check" diluting accountability
  • more of the same: responding to a failure of collective controls by adding more controllers
Control Testing (***)
  • self-certification or inquiry
    limited to secondary controls related to low risk environments
  • examination: supporting evidence and documentation
    moderate assurance and suited for automated controls and sampling of manual controls
  • observation: oversight
  • reperformance (穿行测试)
    strongest form of testing

5.3. Risk Mitigation by Process Design

prevention through design, examples are checklists, standardization, optimized environments or system design

Typology and Mitigation of Human Error (***)
  • skilled-based: slip
    inattention, distraction, environment, tiredness
    controls: adjust pace, improve environment, re-engineer process
  • rule-based: flawed rules
    flawed products, misleading instruction, wrong incentives
  • knowledge-based:
    controls: training, documented procedures, help file, escalation rules
  • violation: voluntary misdeed
    controls: supervisory control, improve culture which reward adherence to rules
Lean Six Sigma

extremely low error rate
DMAIC cycle: define, measure, analyse, improve, control

PDSA Cycle

quality improvement: plan, do, study, act (what changes are to be made in next cycle)

5.4. New Product Approval Process (NPAP)

Its generation is New Initiative Risk Assessment Process (NIRAP).
A proper business case would cover at a minimum five topics:

  1. objective
  2. alternative: reason for making this choise among alternatives
  3. expected benefits
  4. commercial aspects: cost, investment, funding
  5. risks

Risk function during a project life:

  • initial stage: mitigation and monitoring plans
  • project life: update risk
  • project closure: evaluation of project deliverables,

5.5. Mitigation Measures to Reduce Impact

Contingency Planing

emergency plan B, must have a key owner.
Business Continuity Management (BCM)
Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)

Crisis Management and Communication

3 essential qualities: speed, competence, transparency
In crisis mode: firms should also have communication team, which deals with media, stakeholders, including employees.
Four typical phases:

  • emergency response
  • recovery: recovery point objective (RPO) and recovery time objective (RTO)

5.6. Risk Transfer

External Insurance

small losses: captive subsidiary or just absorb the volatility
large losses: cyber risks, major class action lawsuits

Outsourcing

  • For FinTech banks: outsourcing credit risk decisions

Outsourcing can increase or decrease risk, called third-party risk.

Characterisktics and Challenges of Operational Risk Data

A proper allocation of risk management is to focus on large incidents and not to be dispersed by daily volatility.
Wyas to overcome chanllenges:

  • Benchmarking operational losses: report as percentage of gross income / total budget.
  • Averages hide the heterogeneity. Better alternatives are median and the first and third quartiles of the distribution.
Aggregating Qualitative Risk Data

solution:

  • conversion and addition: convert into monetary unit
  • categorization: group by color or score
  • worst-case reporting: potentially over-alarming

7.2. Risk Capital

The objective of BCBS prudential regulation is threefold:

  1. To ensure the solvency and soundness of all financial intermediaries
  2. To provide customers protection from undue risks (failure, fraud, opportunistic behavior)
  3. To promote the efficient and competitive performance of financial instituions

Objective 1 is primary objective, addressed through regulatory capital requirements
Objective 2 and 3 are attained through senior management's competence

In U.S., the focus shifted from capital estimation to stress testing.
Capital is through-the-cycle concept, while stress testing is point-in-time.

Economic Capital: linked to credit rating, influencing cost of funds.

Risk Aggregation for Capital Assessment (*)

intra-risk diversification: diversification within each risk class
operational risk can bring important diversification benefits to capital, because it has low correlation to other financial risks.

7.3. Stress Testing (*)

Taxonomy
  • dimension 1
    • quantitative: sensitivity model to parameter shocks
    • qualitative: scenario analysis such as macro stress testing (multiple parameters), reverse stress testing
  • dimension 2
    • measurable: probabilistic
    • immeasurable: possibilistic

Globally systemically important banks (G-SIBs) are provided a list of macro shock to assess the solvency level.

Reverse Stress Testing

Find how bad an adverse scenario would need to be to achieve a pre-defined outcome, analyze immeasurable risks.
apt for assessing operational resilience

Operational Risk Stress Testing Models

severity can be more complex to model than frequency because of being highly impacted by tail events.
